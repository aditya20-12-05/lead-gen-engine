# Gujarati-language and Gujarati-community media in Africa (as of Oct 2026)

Method note: research done on 2026-10-11 using WebSearch (standard plus extended, including one Gujarati-script query). WebFetch failed with a DNS error (oshwalnairobi.org, `getaddrinfo ENOTFOUND`), so no page in this file was opened in full. Each claim is tagged **VERIFIED** (the search-result snippet supports it, URL given) or **INFERENCE** (my reasoning, not stated in a source). Circulation and audience figures are dated wherever a date was found. Contacts are organisational only.

Main conclusion: **no Gujarati-language newspaper is known to be publishing anywhere in Africa in 2026.** All the Gujarati-script newspapers found are historical (they closed between the 1950s and the 1970s). The people who would read them are now reached through English-language Indian/Asian-community media (Durban's POST and Rising Sun, Kenya's AwaaZ), Asian radio (East FM and Sound Asia in Kenya, Lotus FM and Radio Hindvani in Durban), and community-organisation channels such as Visa Oshwal Nairobi's app and YouTube, Hindu Council of Kenya, and Shree Lohana Mahajan Dar es Salaam.

## Key Question 1: Inventory of historical and current outlets (name, city, publisher, language, frequency, reach, status, finance content, contact route)

### Takeaway
Gujarati print in Africa was a colonial-era and early independence-era phenomenon. Examples are Indian Opinion (Durban, 1903, with a Gujarati section until 1952), Indian Views (Durban), the Tanganyika Herald (Dar es Salaam, about 1929), The Colonial Times (Nairobi, 1933), the Daily Chronicle (Nairobi, 1950–62) and Africa Samachar (Nairobi, 1954–1974). The active outlets in 2026 that reach Gujarati readers are English-language Indian-community titles and Asian radio stations, mainly in Durban and Nairobi/Mombasa.

### Cited Findings

#### A. HISTORICAL outlets (closed)

**Kenya**
- **Africa Samachar**, Nairobi. Publisher: United Africa Press. Weekly, in English and Gujarati, published 1954 to 1974 according to the Library of Congress catalogue. It billed itself as "Africa's most widely circulated Gujarati weekly". VERIFIED — [LOC sn88063890](https://www.loc.gov/item/sn88063890); a 1965 issue from the same publisher is recorded at [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/africa-samachar/oclc/812212166). The 1974 end date comes from the catalogue range, not from a confirmed closure notice. No circulation figure was found.
- **Daily Chronicle**, Nairobi. Daily, in English and Gujarati, 1950 until the 31 May 1962 issue. VERIFIED (LOC catalogue, as summarised in search results) — [LOC Nairobi Gujarati newspapers listing](https://www.loc.gov/newspapers/?all=true&c=150&dates=1962&fa=location:nairobi%7Csubject:nairobi+(kenya)%7Clanguage:gujarati&st=list).
- **The Colonial Times**, Nairobi. Gujarati-English paper co-founded in 1933 by Girdhari Lal Vidyarthi. The colonial government later banned his titles Habari za Dunia (1947) and Jicho (1962). VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Girdhari Lal Vidyarthi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girdhari_Lal_Vidyarthi).
- Academic context: in late-1940s colonial Kenya, Indian and African newspapers published in African vernaculars, Gujarati, Swahili and English, which made official intervention difficult. Some of these papers had print runs of around 10,000. VERIFIED — [Frederiksen, *Africa* (Cambridge), "Print, newspapers and audiences in colonial Kenya"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/africa/article/abs/print-newspapers-and-audiences-in-colonial-kenya-african-and-indian-improvement-protest-and-connections/575C056B4F49EAF103C806989A0C66B8).
- The Nation group was founded by the Aga Khan and was seen by some as an "Asian" paper. VERIFIED — [The EastAfrican](https://www.theeastafrican.co.ke/tea/magazine/the-great-asian-exodus-of-1969-1299736).

**Tanzania**
- **Tanganyika Herald**, Dar es Salaam. Described as an "Anglo-Gujarati daily-weekly journal widely circulated amongst commercial circles", in English and Gujarati, starting about 1929 (the catalogue marks the date with a question mark). Issues from 9 Dec 1930 to 21 Oct 1933 also carry a Gujarati title. Northwestern University holds microfilm for 1930 to 1948, with gaps. VERIFIED — [CRL record](https://www-apps.crl.edu/node/4329).
- Bhanushanker Thaker moved from Gujarat to Dar es Salaam in 1930 and set up a printing press. His partner had earlier launched the Tanganyika Herald. Much of the Asian-owned press output in Tanzania was in English or Gujarati. VERIFIED — [Journal of Eastern African Studies, Ngurumo article, 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17531055.2023.2246762).
- Further reading: Iqbal S. Akhtar, "The Gujarati archive in Tanzania", *History Compass* (2022). VERIFIED that the citation exists (search result only); content not read.

**Uganda**
- **Uganda Herald**, Kampala. An **English-language** tri-weekly published 1912 to 1955, and Uganda's first privately owned commercial newspaper. Its readership was mainly European, with a few Asian and African educated elites. It closed after the Uganda Argus launched in 1955. VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: The Uganda Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Uganda_Herald). This means the brief's description of the Uganda Herald as a Gujarati-community outlet is not supported by the sources.
- **Uganda Argus**, Kampala. Founded in 1955, renamed Voice of Uganda under Amin in 1971, and later succeeded by New Vision. VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Uganda Argus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uganda_Argus).
- No Gujarati-language Ugandan newspaper was found in any search. This is a gap, not proof that none existed.

**South Africa**
- **Indian Opinion**, Durban, later Phoenix Settlement. Founded by Gandhi; the first issue appeared on 4 June 1903. Weekly. Language history from the catalogue: English, Gujarati, Hindi and Tamil from June 1903 to January 1906; English and Gujarati from February 1906 to 1952; English only from 1953. The paper ceased in 1957 (continued as *Opinion*, Phoenix, 1957). Editors included Mansukhlal Nazar, Herbert Kitchin, Henry Polak, Albert West, Manilal Gandhi (editor for 36 years) and Sushila Gandhi. VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Indian Opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Opinion); [mkgandhi.org history](https://mkgandhi.org/articles/history-of-indian-opinion.php); [CRL digital collections](https://digitalcollections.crl.edu/record/613100). Sources conflict on the end dates, so the 1957 date should be treated with some caution.
- **Indian Views**, Durban. Founded in the early twentieth century in opposition to Indian Opinion. It served the **Gujarati-speaking Muslim trading class** across southern Africa and was edited by Moosa Meer from 1929 to 1963. VERIFIED — [Cambridge, *Africa*: "The diaspora at home: Indian Views and the making of Zuleikha Mayat's public voice"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/africa/article/diaspora-at-home-indian-views-and-the-making-of-zuleikha-mayats-public-voice/32AC4C1D92B37F44DC7F8206D0E1E4DA). No closure date was found.
- **The Leader**, Durban. **English-language**, founded by Dhanee Bramdaw on 30 November 1940 for the whole Indian community. VERIFIED — [The Journalist, pioneers series](https://thejournalist.org.za/?p=3710). No closure date was found; it is understood to be defunct (INFERENCE, as no current activity was found).
- **The Graphic**, Durban. Became a weekly tabloid in 1952 and competed with The Leader. VERIFIED — [The Journalist: The Graphic](https://www.thejournalist.org.za/pioneers/the-graphic/).

**Mauritius**
- **Hindusthani**. Launched by Manilal Doctor, initially in English and **Gujarati**, with a Hindi edition added later. It is credited with prompting more than a dozen Hindi papers. Historical; no current status found. VERIFIED — [Orissa Post](https://www.orissapost.com/how-a-hindi-newspaper-backed-by-mahatma-gandhi-triggered-freedom-struggle-in-mauritius/amp/).

### Inferences
- In 2026, the Gujarati-script press in Africa is effectively extinct. Gujarati speakers there read English-language community titles and listen to Hindi/English Asian radio with occasional Gujarati music or segments (INFERENCE from the absence of any active title across about 30 searches and the dates of the historical titles).
- The most cost-effective print and radio reach for Gujarati-heritage readers is in **Durban** (POST, Rising Sun, Lotus FM, Hindvani) and **Nairobi/Mombasa** (East FM, Sound Asia). Everywhere else, community-organisation channels (samaj, mahajan, mandal, jamat newsletters, apps, WhatsApp, events) are the only route (INFERENCE).
- Of the active outlets found, only the radio stations, which run advertising and sponsored talk shows, offer a clear route for a finance columnist or advertiser. No outlet was confirmed to run expert finance columns aimed at this audience (INFERENCE).

## Key Question 2: Where does an affluent Gujarati-speaking audience exist in Africa today, and through which channels is it reachable?

### Takeaway
The largest and most commercially prominent Gujarati-heritage populations are in Kenya (about 90,500 Asians in the 2019 census), Tanzania (about 60,000 Indians, 2015), Uganda (about 20,000–30,000, with outsized economic weight), Zambia, Madagascar (the Khoja-dominated "Karana" business class) and South Africa (Durban/KZN and Gauteng). Media can reach them in Durban and Nairobi. Everywhere else, the realistic routes are community associations, temples, jamats, samaj and mahajan bodies, plus their apps, YouTube channels and events.

### Cited Findings
- **Kenya**: the 2019 census counted 90,527 Asians in total: 47,555 Kenyan citizens and 42,972 non-citizens. The 47,555 figure is corroborated by The Standard. Hindus number 61,287. The Gujarati community is estimated at more than 50,000 (an estimate, not a census figure). The Asian community was recognised as Kenya's 44th tribe in 2017. VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Indians in Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Kenya); [The Standard](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/amp/kenya/article/2001361344/kenyas-biggest-); [Wikipedia: Hinduism in Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Kenya); [Africanews 2017](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2017/07/26/asian-community-in-kenya-get-tribal-status/).
- **Uganda**: estimated at about 30,000 (2016) or 20,000 (2021), down from 80,000–100,000 before the 1972 expulsion. The community is traditionally of Gujarati trader descent and includes the Madhvani and Mehta families and Sudhir Ruparelia, described as Uganda's richest man, whose family is from Gujarat. It is claimed to contribute up to 65% of tax revenue (2016 claim, not verified against official data). VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Indians in Uganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Uganda); [Post SA via PressReader 2021](https://www.pressreader.com/south-africa/post-south-africa/20210407/281681142684188); [The Quint](https://www.thequint.com/amp/story/videos/news-videos/pm-modi-uganda-parliament-speech-indians).
- **Tanzania**: about 60,000 Indians (2015), against more than 150,000 Asians at the peak. The Lohana community is active in Dar es Salaam (2026 event). VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Indians in Tanzania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Tanzania); [Shamlal Puri, Asians in Tanzania](https://ismailimail.blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/asians-in-tanzania-by-shamlal-puri-the-international-indian.pdf); [Guardian TZ 2026](https://www.ippmedia.co.tz/the-guardian/news/local-news/read/religious-summit-points-to-diasporas-widening-function-in-social-welfare-2026-04-09-111153).
- **Zambia**: about 25,000–30,000 Indians, with Lusaka as the hub. Gujaratis arrived from 1905 and work in banking, retail, farming and mining. VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Indians in Zambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Zambia); [Edinburgh thesis on Lusaka Hindus](https://era.ed.ac.uk/items/c309573a-f624-4b38-9c06-30bbbf96b4a8). Joshua Project gives about 53,000 Gujaratis, but it is a missions database with non-neutral framing — [Joshua Project](https://www.joshuaproject.net/index.php/people_groups/11982/ZA).
- **Madagascar**: the "Karana" are mostly Khoja, Ismaili and Bohra Gujarati-origin Muslims centred on Tsaralalana in Antananarivo, and are credited with a large share of trade (claims of 50–60% of the economy are unverified). Population estimates conflict widely, from 17,000 (1994) and 25,000 to 60,000–70,000. They have been targets of looting during unrest. VERIFIED — [Wikipedia: Indians in Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Madagascar); [countrystudies.us](https://countrystudies.us/madagascar/15.htm); [Joshua Project MA](https://www.joshuaproject.net/index.php/people_groups/11982/MA).
- **Zimbabwe**: Hindu Society Harare (founded 1926) is mainly Gujarati, Goan and Tamil, runs two temples and a Gujarati School, and had about 3,000 people in 500 families in 1991. **Botswana**: about 3,353 Hindus (2011 census), concentrated around Gaborone and Selebi-Phikwe. VERIFIED — [hindupad 2023](https://hindupad.com/harare-hindus-plan-wide-ranging-activities-during-2023/); [Hinduism Today 1991](https://hinduismtoday.com/magazine/january-1991/1991-01-modern-temple-rises-out-of-zimbabwe-soil/); [Wikipedia: Hinduism in Botswana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Botswana).
- **South Africa**: there is a South African History Online paper on Gujaratis in South Africa, not read in full — [SAHO PDF](https://www.sahistory.org.za/sites/default/files/Gujaratis%20in%20SA.pdf). Indian-community media are concentrated in Durban and KZN (Chatsworth, Phoenix, Overport) and in Lenasia, Gauteng (see Key Question 1).
- **Mauritius, Malawi, Mozambique, DRC, Nigeria, Ghana**: no reliable source on Gujarati audience size or media channels was found.

### Inferences
- The most affluent and concentrated Gujarati-heritage audiences (INFERENCE from census and business-family evidence) are in Nairobi (Westlands and Parklands), Mombasa, Kampala, Dar es Salaam, Lusaka, Antananarivo and Durban/Johannesburg.
- Channel ranking for reaching that audience (INFERENCE):
  1. Community organisations (Visa Oshwal Nairobi app and YouTube, Hindu Council of Kenya, Shree Lohana Mahajan Dar es Salaam, Hindu Society Harare, Khoja and Bohra jamats in Madagascar)
  2. Asian radio (East FM and Sound Asia in Kenya, Lotus FM and Hindvani in South Africa)
  3. English Indian-community print (POST and POST PLUS, Rising Sun)
  4. Mainstream business press columns (Business Daily Kenya, The Star's Asian column)
- Madagascar's and Zambia's Gujarati business classes appear to be reachable only through community and religious bodies; no media outlet was found (INFERENCE).

### Gaps
- No current wealth or income data specific to Gujarati-heritage households in any country.
- Census figures are dated (Kenya 2019, Tanzania 2015, Uganda 2016–21, Botswana 2011, Zimbabwe 1991).
- No figures were found on Gujarati speakers, as distinct from people of Indian origin.

## Licensing note: does a foreign adviser placing finance columns or ads need a local licence?

(Only what was found in sources. This is not legal advice.)

- **Kenya (CMA)**: investment advisers must be licensed under the Capital Markets Act and the Capital Markets (Licensing Requirements) (General) Regulations 2002. The definition of investment adviser covers anyone making investment recommendations or securities analysis for a fee, **"whether through direct management… or through written publications"**. The CMA publicly warns against unlicensed entities "unlawfully soliciting" investors. Unlicensed financial analysts faced a KSh 500,000 fine or 2 years' imprisonment (a 2017 rule; its current status is unconfirmed). VERIFIED — [policyvault summary of Licensing Regulations](https://policyvault.africa/?p=70756); [serrarigroup on CMA warning](https://serrarigroup.com/cma-flags-15-unlicensed-investment-schemes-in-kenya/); [Business Daily 2017](https://www.businessdailyafrica.com/bd/markets/market-news/cma-backs-financial-analysts-watchdog-clampdown-2156092). INFERENCE: paid investment-recommendation columns aimed at Kenyans are likely to fall within the "written publications" limb. No explicit rule on foreign advertising was found.
- **Uganda (CMA Uganda)**: "no person is permitted to engage in activities regulated under the Act unless duly licensed or approved by the Authority". The CMA publishes an investment adviser licence checklist and a list of approved persons. Unofficial fee figures: UGX 500,000 application and UGX 1,500,000 licence (unverified). VERIFIED — [CMA Uganda](https://cmauganda.co.ug/); [CMA Uganda licence forms](https://cmauganda.co.ug/license-application-renewal-forms); [Eagle Online 2021 public warning on unlicensed financial advice](https://eagle.co.ug/2021/09/01/public-warned-against-dealing-with-unlicensed-persons-offering-financial-advice).
- **Tanzania (CMSA)**: the Capital Markets and Securities (Licensing) Regulations set out dealer and investment adviser licensing, including prescribed forms, audited accounts, fees and a 45-day decision period. No specific rule on foreign promotion was found. VERIFIED (secondary summary) — [regalert.today](https://regalert.today/document/bfee1534-6dc5-4ad1-9669-54d714c247b5/text); [wikiprocedure (unofficial)](https://wikiprocedure.com/index.php/Tanzania_-_Obtain_Investment_Adviser_License).
- **South Africa (FSCA, FAIS)**: "No person may act or offer to act as a FSP within South Africa unless… licensed" under section 8 of FAIS, and this expressly applies to FSPs based outside South Africa. A foreign firm still needs a FAIS licence unless all activity, including marketing to South African investors, occurs offshore. Unsolicited, investor-initiated business is a grey area; active marketing is the clear trigger. Partial exemptions exist, mostly for institutional clients. VERIFIED — [Baker McKenzie guide (2017)](https://resourcehub.bakermckenzie.com/en/resources/global-financial-services-regulatory-guide/europe-middle-east-and-africa/south-africa/topics/how-do-the-licensing-requirements-apply-to-cross-border-business-in-your-jurisdiction); [Webber Wentzel chapter](https://www.webberwentzel.com/About-us/How-we-make-a-difference/Investing-in-South-Africa/Documents/CHAPTER%208%20-%20FINANCIAL%20SERVICES,%20FINANCIAL%20MARKETS%20AND%20MARKET%20ABUSE.pdf); [Wits dissertation 2019](https://wiredspace.wits.ac.za/items/f4276d52-2db0-4e5b-9c06-20eab9115a63). INFERENCE: paid finance ads or columns in POST, Rising Sun or Lotus FM that solicit South African retail clients would likely count as "offering to act" as an FSP.
